# Lease Questions Guide

Written by the Fineprint team as a plain-language companion to official tenant resources.
This guide is educational. It does not say whether any clause is legal or enforceable.
For help with a specific lease, contact UMD Off-Campus Housing or Student Legal Aid.

## Late fees
Typical: Most leases give a short grace period after the due date, then charge a single, fixed late fee.
Worth asking about if: the fee is charged the same day rent is due, there is no grace period, or a daily fee keeps adding up with no cap.
Questions to ask: Is there a grace period? Is the late fee a one-time charge or does it grow each day? Is there a maximum?

## Security deposit
Typical: A refundable deposit is held during the lease and returned after move-out, minus documented charges for damage beyond normal wear and tear. Tenants usually get a move-in condition checklist and an itemized list of any deductions.
Worth asking about if: the deposit amount is unusually high, the lease does not say when it will be returned, or it is called non-refundable.
Questions to ask: When and how will the deposit be returned? Will I get a move-in inspection checklist? Will deductions be itemized?

## Fees
Typical: Some leases list one-time fees such as application or key fees. Fees are usually explained and tied to a specific service.
Worth asking about if: a fee is non-refundable regardless of the apartment's condition, overlaps with what the security deposit already covers, or is vaguely described.
Questions to ask: What exactly does this fee cover? Is any part refundable? How is it different from the security deposit?

## Utilities
Typical: The lease lists which utilities the tenant pays directly and which the landlord provides.
Worth asking about if: the landlord bills a flat utility fee without explaining how it is calculated, or the fee can change without notice.
Questions to ask: How was the flat fee calculated? Can it change during the lease? Can I see past usage?

## Guests and occupancy
Typical: Leases often limit long-term guests so that people living in the unit are on the lease.
Worth asking about if: the guest limit is very short or requires written permission for ordinary visits.
Questions to ask: How do you count guest nights? What is the process if a family member visits for a few weeks?

## Maintenance and repairs
Typical: Landlords usually handle major systems such as plumbing, heating, cooling, electrical, structural items and provided appliances. Tenants are usually responsible for damage they cause and for minor upkeep like replacing light bulbs.
Worth asking about if: the tenant is responsible for all repairs regardless of cause, or for major systems and structural items.
Questions to ask: Who pays if the heat or a provided appliance breaks on its own? How do I submit a repair request and how fast is the response?

## Landlord entry
Typical: Leases usually say the landlord will give advance notice before entering, except in emergencies, and will enter at reasonable times.
Worth asking about if: the landlord may enter at any time, without notice, for any reason.
Questions to ask: How much notice will you give before entering? Can we agree on entry only at reasonable hours except emergencies?

## Alterations
Typical: Painting and installing fixtures need the landlord's permission; small nail holes are often allowed.
Worth asking about if: even ordinary decorating is forbidden or charged heavily.
Questions to ask: What can I hang on the walls? What will I be charged for at move-out?

## Subletting
Typical: Subletting needs landlord approval, and approval is not refused without a reason. Some leases charge a modest processing fee.
Worth asking about if: subletting is banned outright, or the fee is high.
Questions to ask: What is the approval process and how long does it take? What does the fee cover?

## Renewal
Typical: Many leases end on a fixed date or switch to month-to-month. Some renew automatically if neither side gives notice, usually with a notice window of around one to two months and a stated rent for the new term.
Worth asking about if: the lease renews automatically, the notice deadline is very early (for example three or four months before the end), or the new rent is left entirely up to the landlord.
Questions to ask: What is the exact date I must give notice by? Can you remind me in writing before that date? How will the renewal rent be set?

## Early termination
Typical: Leases describe what happens if a tenant leaves early, such as paying a defined fee or rent until a new tenant is found.
Worth asking about if: all remaining rent becomes due at once and an extra lease-break fee is added on top.
Questions to ask: If I need to leave early, will you try to re-rent the unit? Is there a single defined buy-out fee instead?

## Liability
Typical: Leases often say the landlord is not responsible for tenants' personal property and encourage renter's insurance.
Worth asking about if: the landlord is not responsible for any injury or damage from any cause, including the landlord's own negligence.
Questions to ask: What happens if something in the building that you maintain causes damage or injury? Can this clause be narrowed to tenants' personal property?

## Joint and several liability
Typical: Common in shared apartments. Each roommate can be held responsible for the full rent and any damage, not just their share.
Worth asking about if: roommates have not agreed among themselves how they will handle a roommate who leaves.
Questions to ask (for roommates): Should we sign a roommate agreement? What happens if one of us moves out early?

## Renter's insurance
Typical: Many leases require or encourage renter's insurance for personal property and liability.
Questions to ask: Is insurance required? What minimum coverage do you expect?

## Move-in and move-out
Typical: Tenants do a move-in inspection, return keys at move-out, and give a forwarding address so the deposit can be returned.
Questions to ask: Can we do a walk-through together at move-in and move-out? Where will my deposit be sent?
